# Acknowledgements

This project depends on generations of historians, archivists, curators, engineers, collectors, conservators, oral-history interviewers, librarians, and volunteers who preserved machines and the paper trails around them.

`computing-archaeology` is not an attempt to replace their work. It is possible only because that work exists.

## Institutions and collections currently used

### Computer History Museum (CHM)

A major foundation for the initial excavation set.

The repository currently cites CHM material on:

- Charles Babbage's Difference and Analytical Engines;
- George Stibitz and Bell Labs computing;
- ENIAC and early electronic computers;
- EDSAC and delay-line memory;
- Williams–Kilburn tube memory;
- magnetic drums and magnetic-core memory;
- Jay Forrester;
- IBM Project Stretch archival documents.

Particular thanks are due to the people who preserve and make accessible the museum's **Timeline of Computer History**, **Revolution** exhibit, **The Storage Engine**, Babbage materials, oral histories, scanned manuals, and archival documents.

https://computerhistory.org/

### IBM History

IBM's historical pages are used in the first set of articles for the history of:

- Herman Hollerith's tabulating system;
- punched-card data processing;
- the IBM punched-card ecosystem.

Corporate histories are cited as institutional sources rather than treated as neutral final authorities. Their value is especially high when they point to machines, business practices, internal chronology, and surviving company records that can be checked against independent scholarship.

https://www.ibm.com/history

## People whose work appears in the first excavation set

The initial material necessarily builds on the work of historical engineers and researchers including, among many others:

- **Charles Babbage** — Difference and Analytical Engine designs;
- **Joseph Clement** — precision engineering and construction work for Difference Engine No. 1;
- **Ada Lovelace** — published notes on the Analytical Engine and its possible symbolic reach;
- **Herman Hollerith** — punched-card statistical data processing;
- **George Stibitz** — relay calculation and remote computing demonstration;
- **J. Presper Eckert** and **John Mauchly** — early electronic computing and memory work;
- **Maurice Wilkes** and the EDSAC team — practical stored-program computing service and delay-line systems;
- **Frederic Williams**, **Tom Kilburn**, and colleagues at Manchester — Williams–Kilburn storage and the Manchester Baby;
- **Jay W. Forrester** and the Whirlwind team — practical magnetic-core memory and real-time computing;
- **Werner Buchholz** and the IBM Project Stretch team — word-length, byte, and architecture design work.

This list is not a claim that these systems were created by isolated individuals. Large computing projects were collective engineering efforts involving many people whose names are often less visible in popular histories.

## Invisible labor matters

Computing history is especially vulnerable to narratives that celebrate designers while erasing the labor that made systems work.

Future additions should actively recover, where sources permit:

- operators;
- programmers;
- wiring and assembly workers;
- keypunch operators;
- technicians;
- maintenance staff;
- production workers;
- magnetic-core weavers and inspectors;
- clerical workers;
- documentation writers;
- standards participants;
- users whose workflows shaped the machines.

Acknowledgement should not be ceremonial. Labor, maintenance, and institutional practice are part of the causal history of computing.

## Historians of computing

The project should increasingly cite scholarship from the professional history-of-computing community, including work published in venues such as **IEEE Annals of the History of Computing**, the Charles Babbage Institute, museum research programs, university archives, and scholarly monographs.

As the repository grows, this file should evolve from a general acknowledgement into a more explicit map of collections and historians whose work supports each major topic.
