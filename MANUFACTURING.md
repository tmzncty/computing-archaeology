# Manufacturing Is Part of Computing History

A computer history that jumps from transistor invention to integrated circuits and then to microprocessors skips the industrial substrate that made the transition possible.

This repository treats manufacturing as a first-class historical track.

## Why this deserves its own track

The computer industry depends on other industries that ordinary architecture histories can make invisible:

```text
high-purity semiconductor materials
crystal growth and wafer preparation
oxidation and diffusion
photoresist / mask making / lithography
etch and deposition
cleanrooms and contamination control
wafer probing and automatic test
wire bonding and packaging
copper-clad laminates
PCB imaging / etching / drilling / plating
component insertion / soldering / reflow
inspection / repair / field service
```

These are not peripheral production details. They determine what architectures can be manufactured reliably and sold cheaply enough to matter.

## Next excavations

This track should continue into:

- zone refining and Czochralski crystal growth;
- wafer diameter and why larger wafers matter;
- mask shops, rubylith, step-and-repeat cameras, steppers, and scanners;
- ion implantation and epitaxy;
- cleanroom history and particle metrology;
- statistical process control and parametric test;
- wire bonding, lead frames, ceramic/plastic packages, and thermal packaging;
- PCB laminates including phenolic materials and FR-4;
- plated-through-hole and multilayer board production;
- wave soldering, surface mount, solder paste, pick-and-place, and reflow;
- automated optical inspection and in-circuit test;
- connectors, sockets, backplanes, cables, and power distribution;
- the geographic and labor history of semiconductor assembly and PCB production;
- the equipment industry beneath fabs and board houses;
- the transition from integrated device manufacturers to foundry/fabless ecosystems.

> **A billion-transistor processor is not only a triumph of logic design. It is evidence that an enormous manufacturing civilization learned to repeat microscopic structures with extraordinary reliability.**
