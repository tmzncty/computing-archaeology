# Manufacturing Field Set 4 — Acknowledgements

This field set depends on people whose work is especially easy to erase because their success appears as a clean specification, a green test bin, an intact solder joint, or a wafer lot that quietly reaches the next operation.

## Process-control and yield work

Credit belongs to the people who made semiconductor processes observable:

- metrology technicians;
- SPC/process-control engineers;
- parametric-test engineers;
- process-monitor/test-structure designers;
- wafer-map and yield analysts;
- excursion-review teams;
- process-integration engineers;
- failure-analysis laboratories;
- technicians maintaining measurement calibration and standards.

A control chart, wafer map, or hold decision is part of the manufactured chip's history even though it never appears in the product data sheet.

## Probe, screening, and test interfaces

This field set also depends on:

- probe-card designers and technicians;
- automatic-prober operators and field-service engineers;
- ATE/test-program engineers;
- test-floor operators;
- handler/socket technicians;
- burn-in-board designers;
- burn-in chamber operators;
- reliability-screening and qualification staff;
- binning and outgoing-quality teams.

A die becomes a saleable product partly because these workers convert an uncertain population into measured categories.

## Reliability and materials failure

Thanks are due to the researchers and technicians who documented failures that product histories often omit:

- metallization and electromigration researchers;
- package cross-section and microscopy technicians;
- solder-fatigue and thermal-cycle test teams;
- materials scientists studying CTE mismatch and creep;
- delamination/moisture/reliability analysts;
- destructive-physical-analysis staff;
- reliability-data archivists.

NASA/JPL technical-report preservation is particularly valuable because it keeps photographs, test procedures, failed hardware, and negative results visible instead of preserving only successful products.

## Inspection and board-test labor

Dense PCB assembly depends on a second observation system:

- AOI programmers and review operators;
- machine-vision/lighting engineers;
- ICT fixture designers and bed-of-nails technicians;
- flying-probe/test engineers;
- X-ray and acoustic-inspection operators;
- rework technicians;
- defect classifiers;
- board-house quality engineers.

Automation does not eliminate inspection labor. It redistributes it into programming, calibration, false-call review, fixture maintenance, and failure classification.

## FOUP, MES, AMHS, and factory-state work

A highly automated fab is maintained by people who keep physical and digital state synchronized:

- FOUP/carrier maintenance staff;
- load-port and robot technicians;
- overhead/automated material-handling engineers;
- equipment-integration engineers;
- SECS/GEM/GEM300 implementers;
- MES developers and operators;
- dispatch/scheduling engineers;
- recipe-governance staff;
- lot/wafer genealogy and traceability specialists;
- recovery teams resolving stuck, misidentified, held, or suspect material.

A wafer fab can be automated only because these people maintain identities, state machines, interfaces, and exception procedures.

## Layout-data and mask-data work

The words `tape-out` and `GDSII` hide another production chain:

- CAD librarians;
- physical-layout data-preparation staff;
- layer-map and revision-control workers;
- mask-data/fracture engineers;
- reticle job-deck preparation;
- mask-writer operators;
- reticle inspection and repair technicians;
- MOSIS/multi-project-wafer service staff;
- archive maintainers who preserved Calma manuals and early CAD documentation.

Thanks are especially due to **Bitsavers**, **Al Kossow**, the **Computer History Museum**, and the communities preserving Calma, Mead–Conway, and MOSIS records.

## Advanced-packaging work

Multi-die systems add another manufacturing workforce:

- bump/UBM process engineers;
- substrate and interposer designers;
- TSV/process-integration teams;
- underfill/materials engineers;
- die-bond/flip-chip operators;
- package-warpage and thermal engineers;
- known-good-die/test teams;
- X-ray/acoustic/package inspection;
- thermal-cycle/reliability qualification;
- package yield and rework specialists.

The package is architecture only because a manufacturing organization can repeatedly assemble, inspect, and qualify it.

Particular thanks in this field set go to:

- **SEMI** and semiconductor-equipment/factory-automation participants for preserving oral histories and standards evolution around probing, equipment communication, FOUP, GEM300, and traceability;
