# Manufacturing Track Acknowledgements

The manufacturing history in this repository exists because technical workers, archivists, historians, museums, standards bodies, military laboratories, corporate archives, collectors, and independent scanners preserved evidence that is unusually easy to lose.

## Collections and institutions

Particular thanks for this first field set go to:

- the **Computer History Museum**, especially *The Silicon Engine* and its preservation of semiconductor process milestones, patents, oral histories, artifacts, and technical references;
- **Bell Labs / Nokia Bell Labs** archives for transistor, diffusion, oxide, and semiconductor process history;
- the **U.S. Army Communications-Electronics Command (CECOM)** historical program for preserving Signal Corps printed-circuit and Auto-Sembly history;
- the **National Bureau of Standards / NIST** lineage whose postwar printed-circuit publications documented an industry while it was still forming;
- **Bitsavers**, **Al Kossow**, and independent manual scanners for keeping semiconductor, packaging, computer-module, and PCB-adjacent documentation accessible;
- patent and archival services that preserve early process disclosures long after the original manufacturing lines disappeared.

## Manufacturing labor

This track should not repeat the common pattern in which a process is credited only to a named inventor while the people who made it reproducible disappear.

The semiconductor and PCB industries depended on people doing work such as:

```text
crystal growth
wafer slicing / lapping / polishing
furnace loading
oxidation / diffusion
mask making
photoresist coating
alignment / exposure
etch / deposition
wafer cleaning
cleanroom operation
metrology
probe testing
failure analysis
wire bonding
lead-frame assembly
encapsulation
final test / binning
laminate production
copper-foil handling
PCB imaging and etching
drilling / routing
plating
component insertion
soldering / wave-solder operation
inspection
rework
field service
```

Many semiconductor assembly and inspection operations historically relied heavily on women and on geographically dispersed production labor that is underrepresented in product-centered histories.

## Engineers are not the whole factory

Named researchers including **Morris Tanenbaum, Gordon Teal, Calvin Fuller, Carl Frosch, Lincoln Derick, Jules Andrus, Walter Bond, Jean Hoerni, Robert Noyce, Jay Last, Gordon Moore, Federico Faggin**, and many others are important because surviving documents let us connect ideas to process changes.

But none of those names substitutes for the process engineers, technicians, operators, equipment builders, chemical suppliers, mask workers, test engineers, packaging workers, board-house workers, and maintenance crews that turned those ideas into production.

## PCB preservation

Printed-circuit history is especially fragmented across:

- military reports;
- patents;
- NBS/NIST publications;
- trade magazines;
- board-house records;
- equipment vendors;
- oral histories;
- surviving boards and modules.

Thanks are due to the people who save old boards rather than stripping them only for components, because trace geometry, laminate, plating, soldering, connectors, and repair marks can preserve manufacturing evidence that the schematic does not.
