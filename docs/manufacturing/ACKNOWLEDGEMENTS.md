# Manufacturing Track Acknowledgements

The manufacturing history in this repository exists because technical workers, archivists, historians, museums, standards bodies, military laboratories, corporate archives, collectors, equipment vendors, board houses, and independent scanners preserved evidence that is unusually easy to lose.

## Collections and institutions

Particular thanks go to:

- the **Computer History Museum**, especially *The Silicon Engine* and its preservation of semiconductor process milestones, patents, oral histories, artifacts, equipment history, mask artwork, packaging, MOS, memory, and technical references;
- **Bell Labs / Nokia Bell Labs** archives for transistor, zone-refining, crystal-growth, diffusion, oxide, MOS, epitaxy, and semiconductor-process history;
- the **U.S. Army Communications-Electronics Command (CECOM)** historical program for preserving Signal Corps printed-circuit, dip-solder, Auto-Sembly, and military miniaturization history;

- **Bitsavers**, **Al Kossow**, and independent scanners for preserving manufacturer data books, packaging literature, test-equipment documents, PCB-adjacent records, and contemporary surface-mount proceedings;

- **TSMC's historical annual reports and company records** for documenting the foundry model the company presented to customers and investors, again as corporate self-description rather than neutral industrial history;
- patent, standards, university, trade-journal, and specialist archival services that preserve process disclosures after the original production lines and equipment generations disappear.

## The people upstream of the wafer

A finished wafer hides a long chain before fabrication begins:

```text
raw-material purification
zone refining
crystal pulling / float-zone growth
dopant preparation
crystal inspection
wafer slicing
lapping / polishing
cleaning
wafer specification / metrology
```

This track should preserve the work of crystal growers, chemists, furnace builders, wafer-polishing workers, materials technicians, and suppliers whose names rarely appear in processor histories.

## Mask shops and lithography labor

Early IC patterning depended on people who:

- drafted layouts at enlarged scale;
- cut rubylith artwork;
- photographed and reduced patterns;
- built and maintained step-and-repeat cameras;
- prepared masks;
- inspected mask defects;
- aligned wafers;
- coated and developed resist;
- measured overlay and critical dimensions;
- repaired or replaced damaged masks.

The lithography process is not only optics. It is a production organization.

## Fab operations and equipment work

Semiconductor manufacturing depends on people doing work such as:

```text
furnace loading

implant operation
etch / deposition
wafer cleaning
cleanroom operation
chemical delivery
vacuum maintenance
metrology
probe testing
parametric test
failure analysis
yield engineering
equipment installation
field service
spare-parts logistics
process integration
```

Special credit belongs to equipment engineers and technicians at companies that turned one-off laboratory apparatus into repeatable commercial tools. A fab can only standardize a process when furnaces, aligners, deposition tools, implanters, probers, bonders, and test systems are themselves manufacturable and serviceable.

## Packaging and assembly labor

The die still has to survive contact with the outside world.

That depends on:

- die attach;
- wire bonding;
- lead-frame handling;
- ceramic/plastic package production;
- molding / sealing;
- package marking;
- burn-in;
- final electrical test;
- binning;
- failure analysis.

Packaging workers and test operators are part of the computer's manufacturing history, not a postscript.

## PCB and surface-mount labor

Board production adds another manufacturing civilization:

```text
laminate production
copper-foil handling
imaging / resist
etching
drilling / routing
plating
multilayer layup / lamination
solder mask / legend
stencil preparation
solder-paste printing
feeder setup
pick-and-place
wave solder / reflow
AOI / X-ray / ICT
inspection
rework
field repair
```

Surface mount replaces some visible manual operations with precision placement, paste, thermal processing, and inspection. Automation does not eliminate labor; it changes its form.

## Foundry and customer-engineering work

The foundry/fabless boundary creates another class of technical labor that ordinary chip histories often omit:

- process design kit maintenance;
- design-rule support;
- device-model characterization;
- customer tapeout support;
- mask-data preparation;
- design-for-manufacturing review;
- package/test coordination;
- yield correlation;
- failure analysis across company boundaries;
- capacity planning and production control.

A “fabless” chip is not manufacturing-less. It depends on a formal manufacturing interface maintained by many people.

## Preservation matters

Manufacturing evidence is especially fragile because companies discard old masks, process travelers, wafer maps, recipe sheets, calibration logs, test programs, equipment manuals, fixtures, and boards when a line is retired.

Thanks are due to people who save:

- old wafers and packages;
- obsolete board assemblies;
- process and equipment manuals;
- trade journals;
- mask artwork;
- factory photographs;
- oral histories from production workers;
- failure-analysis records;
- standards revisions;
- corporate annual reports;
- equipment brochures and service documentation.

These materials preserve the difference between a schematic idea and a manufacturable product.
