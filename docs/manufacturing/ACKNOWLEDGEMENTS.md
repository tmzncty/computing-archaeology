# Manufacturing Track Acknowledgements

The manufacturing history in this repository exists because technical workers, archivists, historians, museums, standards bodies, military laboratories, corporate archives, collectors, equipment vendors, board houses, and independent scanners preserved evidence that is unusually easy to lose.

## The people upstream of the wafer

A finished wafer hides a long chain before fabrication begins:

```text
raw-material purification
zone refining
crystal pulling / float-zone growth
dopant preparation
crystal inspection
wafer slicing
lapping / polishing
cleaning
wafer specification / metrology
```

This track should preserve the work of crystal growers, chemists, furnace builders, wafer-polishing workers, materials technicians, and suppliers whose names rarely appear in processor histories.

## Mask shops, lithography, and reticle labor

IC patterning depended and still depends on people who:

- drafted layouts at enlarged scale;
- cut rubylith artwork;
- photographed and reduced patterns;
- prepared and wrote masks/reticles;
- inspected defects;
- aligned wafers;
- operated contact/projection/step-and-repeat tools;
- coated and developed resist;
- measured overlay and critical dimensions;
- calibrated stages and optics;
- repaired or replaced damaged masks;
- maintained reticle libraries and revision control.

The lithography process is not only optics. It is a production organization and a precision machine-tool industry.

Semiconductor manufacturing depends on people doing work such as:

```text
anneal

chemical delivery
vacuum maintenance
```

Special credit belongs to equipment engineers and technicians who turned one-off laboratory apparatus into repeatable commercial tools. A fab can only standardize a process when furnaces, aligners, deposition tools, implanters, probers, bonders, robots, and test systems are themselves manufacturable and serviceable.

## Wafer probe, automatic test, and yield work

A shipped specification hides another population of workers and software:

- probe-card technicians;
- wafer-probe operators;
- ATE operators;
- test-program engineers;
- calibration technicians;
- binning/yield engineers;
- parametric-test staff;
- wafer-map analysts;
- failure-analysis laboratories;
- reliability engineers;
- burn-in and final-test operators.

These people turn statistical wafer output into products that can be sold with definite electrical specifications.

The test code and limits they maintain are part of semiconductor manufacturing even though none of that software ships inside the final processor.

## Packaging, wire bonding, and hidden metallurgy

The die still has to survive contact with the outside world.

That depends on:

- die attach;
- bond-pad preparation;
- thermocompression / ultrasonic / thermosonic bonding;
- wire-bond inspection and pull testing;
- lead-frame handling;
- ceramic/plastic package production;
- molding / sealing;
- solder-bump and flip-chip processing;
- underfill and thermal materials;
- package marking;
- X-ray / acoustic inspection;
- burn-in;
- final electrical test;
- destructive physical analysis.

Packaging workers and reliability technicians are part of the computer's manufacturing history, not a postscript. The history of “purple plague” is a reminder that a microscopic metal interface can determine whether an otherwise perfect chip survives.

## PCB and surface-mount labor

```text
laminate production
copper-foil handling

etching

plating
multilayer layup / lamination
solder mask / legend
```

Surface mount replaces some visible manual operations with precision placement, paste, thermal processing, and inspection. Automation does not eliminate labor; it changes its form.

## SMIF, robots, and factory-material handling

When wafers moved into sealed pods and local minienvironments, another set of workers became essential:

- carrier/pod maintenance;
- load-port technicians;
- wafer-handling robot maintenance;
- automated material-handling engineers;
- lot-tracking/MES support;
- equipment-integration engineers;
- contamination-control staff;
- recovery teams for handling faults without damaging high-value wafer lots.

The automated fab is still operated and repaired by people; it simply moves routine wafer contact behind standardized interfaces.

## Design-rule, EDA, mask-data, and foundry interface work

The foundry/fabless boundary creates technical labor that ordinary chip histories often omit:

- process design kit maintenance;
- scalable/design-rule definition;
- design-rule checking deck maintenance;
- transistor-model characterization;
- CAD/EDA tool development;
- CAD librarians;
- mask-data preparation;
- multi-project wafer coordination;
- MOSIS service operations;
- customer tapeout support;
- design-for-manufacturing review;
- package/test coordination;
- yield correlation;
- failure analysis across company boundaries;
- capacity planning and production control.

A “fabless” chip is not manufacturing-less. It depends on a formal manufacturing interface maintained by many people.

## Preservation matters

Manufacturing evidence is especially fragile because companies discard old masks, process travelers, wafer maps, recipe sheets, calibration logs, test programs, probe cards, equipment manuals, fixtures, carriers, and boards when a line is retired.

Thanks are due to people who save:

- old wafers and packages;
- obsolete board assemblies;
- process and equipment manuals;
- trade journals;
- mask artwork and reticles;
- factory photographs;
- oral histories from production workers;
- failure-analysis records;
- standards revisions;
- corporate annual reports;
- tester programs and fixtures;
- carrier/automation documentation;
- equipment brochures and service documentation.

These materials preserve the difference between a schematic idea and a manufacturable product.
