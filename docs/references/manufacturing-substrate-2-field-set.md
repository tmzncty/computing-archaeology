# Manufacturing Substrate — Field Set 2 Source Map

This source map supports the second manufacturing-history excavation set: semiconductor purity/crystal growth, mask making, MOS maturation, EPROM, equipment vendors, wafer scaling, foundry/fabless organization, and surface-mount board assembly.

Article footnotes remain the claim-level map. This file records source type, strengths, and cautions.

## 1. Semiconductor purity and crystal growth

### Computer History Museum — zone refining

- “Development of Zone Refining”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/development-of-zone-refining/

Use for William Pfann, Henry Theurer, Bell Labs zone-refining context, and the relationship between ultra-pure material and controlled doping.

Source type: museum synthesis pointing to Bell Labs technical literature and oral history.

### W. G. Pfann / Bell Laboratories

- “Single Crystals of Exceptional Perfection and Uniformity by Zone Leveling,” 1956: https://www.nokia.com/bell-labs/publications-and-media/publications/single-crystals-of-exceptional-perfection-and-uniformity-by-zone-leveling/

Use as near-primary technical evidence for zone-melting concepts and extremely high semiconductor purity.

### Computer History Museum — crystal growth

- “First Grown-Junction Transistors Fabricated”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/first-grown-junction-transistors-fabricated/
- “Bell Labs Licenses Transistor Technology”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/bell-labs-licenses-transistor-technology/
- “People”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/people/

Use for Gordon Teal, John Little, Ernest Buehler, Morgan Sparks, the adaptation of the Czochralski method, float-zone work, and the industrial importance of large single crystals.

Caution: do not compress the history into “Czochralski invented semiconductor crystal growth.” His 1917 technique was adapted decades later by teams building semiconductor production methods.

## 2. Photolithography, masks, and step-and-repeat

### Computer History Museum

- “Photolithography Techniques Are Used to Make Silicon Devices”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/photolithography-techniques-are-used-to-make-silicon-devices/

Use for:

- Andrus/Bond adapting printed-circuit photoengraving;
- photoresist and oxide-window patterning;
- Lathrop/Nall work;
- Fairchild's early step-and-repeat camera;
- GCA/Mann commercial photo-repeaters;
- preserved rubylith mask artwork.

Source type: museum synthesis with explicit patents, papers, oral histories, and artifact images.

Priority caution: lithography was an accumulated technology spanning printing, PCBs, military microelectronics, optics, chemistry, and semiconductor process development.

## 3. MOS manufacturing maturation

### First MOS device

- CHM, “Metal Oxide Semiconductor (MOS) Transistor Demonstrated”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/metal-oxide-semiconductor-mos-transistor-demonstrated/

Use for Atalla/Kahng and the silicon/silicon-dioxide interface.

### Early commercial MOS and instability

- CHM, “First Commercial MOS IC Introduced”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/first-commercial-mos-ic-introduced/

Use for the gap between demonstrated MOS and stable high-volume MOS manufacturing, including 1960s oxide/interface/reliability problems.

The page points toward contemporary work by Grove, Deal, Snow, Sah, and others.

### Silicon-gate MOS

- CHM, “Silicon Gate Technology Developed for ICs”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/silicon-gate-technology-developed-for-ics/

Use for Kerwin/Klein/Sarace, Faggin/Klein, the Fairchild 3708, self-alignment, Intel's silicon-gate manufacturing platform, and links to the 4004 and EPROM.

### MOS and DRAM economics

- CHM, “MOS Dynamic RAM Competes with Magnetic Core Memory on Price”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/mos-dynamic-ram-competes-with-magnetic-core-memory-on-price/

Use for semiconductor-memory cost pressure and the role of later process innovations including ion implantation.

## 4. EPROM and firmware iteration

### Intel institutional histories

- Intel, “A Success…Out of Quality Control Issues”: https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/history/virtual-vault/articles/eprom.html
- Intel timeline, “The World's First EPROM: The 1702”: https://timeline.intel.com/1971/the-world%27s-first-eprom%3A-the-1702

Use for Intel's account of Dov Frohman's work, UV erasure, the quartz-window package, and the development-feedback value of erasable nonvolatile memory.

Source type: corporate history. Useful for Intel's own chronology and framing, but priority and broad market-impact claims require independent corroboration.

### Computer History Museum

- “Pioneers of Semiconductor Non-Volatile Memory (NVM): The First Four Decades”: https://computerhistory.org/blog/pioneers-of-semiconductor-non-volatile-memory-nvm-the-first-four-decades/

Use for broader floating-gate/nonvolatile-memory context, competitors, OTP packaging, and the EPROM→EEPROM→flash lineage.

### Equipment suppliers

- CHM, “Turnkey Equipment Suppliers Change Industry Dynamics”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/turnkey-equipment-suppliers-change-industry-dynamics/

Use for:

- Thermco diffusion furnaces;
- GCA/Mann and Perkin-Elmer lithography;
- Kulicke & Soffa aligners/bonding context;
- Electroglas wafer probing;
- Varian vacuum/implantation equipment;
- Applied Materials epitaxial/CVD systems;

## 6. Wafer diameter and factory scale

Current anchor:

- CHM, “Turnkey Equipment Suppliers Change Industry Dynamics”: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/turnkey-equipment-suppliers-change-industry-dynamics/

The page explicitly connects wafer-diameter growth to front-end equipment evolution.

Future primary-source targets:

- wafer-supplier specifications;
- SEMI standards for wafer dimensions, flats/notches, carriers, and automation;
- fab-conversion announcements;
- 100/150/200/300 mm tool manuals;
- crystal-grower records;
- cost/yield studies tied to specific transitions.

Do not write “larger wafer = cheaper chips” without specifying process, die size, tool cost, yield, wafer cost, and utilization assumptions.

- tool installation/field-service engineers;

- reflow and wave-solder operators;
- AOI/inspection/rework workers;
- process-integration and yield engineers;
- foundry customer-engineering teams.

## General caution

The manufacturing substrate is highly cumulative.

Avoid stories of the form:

> Person X invented process Y, therefore modern chips became possible.

Prefer chains that show how materials, tools, recipes, standards, labor, testing, and economic organization had to mature together.
