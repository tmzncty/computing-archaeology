# Manufacturing Substrate Field Set — Source Map

This source map supports the semiconductor / packaging / PCB excavation round. Article footnotes remain the finest-grained claim-to-source map.

The central research rule for this field set is:

> **Do not confuse an invention with a manufacturable industry.**

A working device, a repeatable process, high-volume yield, reliable packaging, and economical system assembly are different historical achievements.

  https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/moores-law-predicts-the-future-of-integrated-circuits/

  https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/timeline/

Use Moore's 1965 history carefully: the famous projection was tied to economics and minimum cost per component, not merely an isolated transistor-count prophecy.

The experiments in this repository use synthetic yield equations and probabilities. They must never be cited as period fab measurements.

## 5. Fabs, process equipment, and production knowledge

## 7. Printed circuits and Auto-Sembly

### U.S. Army / CECOM historical archive

- Susan Thompson, “Historical Innovations pave the way for current microelectronic mission at CECOM,” U.S. Army Communications-Electronics Command, 2026  
  https://www.army.mil/article/290502/historical_innovations_pave_the_way_for_current_microelectronic_mission_at_cecom

Source type: recent institutional history explicitly drawing on CECOM historical archives.

Important details include:

- National Bureau of Standards Circular 468 (1947) describing printed circuits as already beyond experimental status;
- postwar Signal Corps printed/etched circuit work;
- Moe Abramson and Stanislaus F. Danko's 1949 Auto-Sembly process;
- component insertion through holes and batch soldering;
- 1956 U.S. Army patent.

Follow these references into the original NBS circular, patent, and 1951 *Electronics* article when precise priority or process details matter.

Use for the board's dual role as mechanical support and electrical interconnect and for modern terminology around traces, pads, planes, and substrates.

## 9. Historical caution around PCB priority

The history of printed wiring includes multiple strands:

- early printed/stamped/plated conductor patents;
- Paul Eisler's practical printed-circuit work;
- proximity-fuze production;
- National Bureau of Standards dissemination;
- Army Signal Corps subtractive fabrication and Auto-Sembly;
- double-sided boards;
- plated-through-hole techniques;
- multilayer lamination;
- wave soldering;
- surface-mount processes.

Do **not** collapse this into “one person invented the PCB in year X” without defining which technical criterion is meant.

## 10. Labor and industrial history targets

Future research should actively seek sources on:

### Semiconductor production

- crystal-growing operators;
- diffusion-furnace operators;
- mask/layout workers;
- wafer cleaning and handling;
- cleanroom operators;
- probe/test technicians;
- reliability/quality teams;
- assembly and wire-bonding labor;
- packaging workers;
- equipment maintenance;
- chemical/gas/facilities workers.

### PCB production

- artwork/layout departments;
- laminate and copper-foil production;
- imaging/etching workers;
- drilling and routing;
- plating lines;
- component insertion;
- soldering and wave-solder operation;
- inspection and electrical test;
- repair/rework;
- connector and cable assembly.

Popular computing history routinely attributes products to chip designers while hiding the manufacturing labor that converted layouts into reliable volume production.

## 11. Relationship to the previous 6502 field set

The repository's 6502 article already argues that price targets can constrain allowable die area and architecture using Chuck Peddle / Bill Mensch participant evidence.

This manufacturing field set supplies the lower layers under that argument:

```text
die area
-> defect exposure / die per wafer
-> yield
-> test / package cost
-> selling price
-> feasible architecture and market
```

The exact historical numbers must come from product/fab records when available; the generic chain is an engineering reconstruction.

## Source-handling checklist

For manufacturing claims, ask:

1. Is this a laboratory demonstration or production process?
2. Is the source describing a device, a wafer process, packaging, or system assembly?
3. Is a quoted yield/cost figure from the actual period and factory?
4. Is the source an inventor account, corporate history, patent, factory record, trade article, or later synthesis?
5. Does “first” mean first concept, first working device, first production process, or first high-volume commercial use?
6. Are invisible manufacturing and maintenance workers being erased by an inventor-centered narrative?
7. Is a modern process term being projected backward onto an earlier factory that used different methods?

A manufacturing history is credible only when it separates invention, process integration, scaling, yield, packaging, assembly, and labor.
